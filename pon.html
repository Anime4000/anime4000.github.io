Why it matters

An operator can mandate its own ONT, lock the firmware, keep a management channel open into your house and refuse equipment you supply yourself. In most of the world there is no rule that says it cannot. The EU gives end users the right to use terminal equipment of their choice; Germany has had it in law since 2016 and Italy has its modem libero rules. Malaysia has no equivalent, and neither do most of the countries where this community lives.

Where the rules already protect the subscriber, open firmware is a convenience. Where they do not, it is the only leverage a subscriber has.

The ask is modest: that the demarcation be honest. The operator's responsibility ends at the fibre, what runs on the subscriber's side is the subscriber's choice, and equipment somebody paid for belongs to them.

RTL960x ／ ハッキング

Where it started: hacking RTL960x

RTL960x documents and demystifies Realtek's GPON platform for the global xPON community: how to set up a stick, clone the identity of a stock ONU, pick the right LAN mode for 2.5GbE, read OMCI, recover from a bad flash, and which ISPs need which tweak.

It is the work of many hands. People in Malaysia, Italy, Ukraine, Taiwan, Vietnam, Indonesia and a dozen other places test against their own operators and send back what they find. I keep it together.

Safety notice ／ 注意

Open networking requires responsibility

PON is a shared medium. One misbehaving ONU can disrupt upstream synchronisation for every subscriber on the same fibre split, and operators watch for exactly that — disconnection, blacklisting and penalties are real outcomes.

This work promotes open knowledge and interoperability, not reckless deployment. A live network is not a place to experiment. Do not endanger shared infrastructure, do not disrupt others, and proceed with discipline.
